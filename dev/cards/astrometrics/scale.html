Scale ​

An Astrometrics card can be drawn to look right or to be right. Both are the same card with the same keys.

  • Built to fit (most starters): sizes and distances picked so everything shows at once. Planets are big enough to see from across their system, a station is a sizeable part of its planet. Easy to read, nothing to tune, and not to scale.
  • True scale: the real numbers, so what you see is how it is: a solar system in km where planets are dots from across it, a galaxy in light years with a station 1.5 km across inside it. The close views are where it shines; you pick what shows from afar with the distance keys.
  • The Solar system (real positions) and Galaxy to station at true scale starters are ready-made true-scale scenes.

Units ​

units on the card names what one scene unit is, for real data: m, km, au, ly or pc. Positions, sizes and distances are all in scene units; without a name they are your own.

  • The name tells the card how a star system inside relates to the scene, draws a model at its real size, places real bodies (body: points) at their real distances and gives a galaxy the Milky Way's radius in those units.
  • It does not rescale what is already there: naming the units of a scene built in scene units only says what its numbers mean.
  • Screen sizes (line widths, label sizes, marker sizes) are in px whatever the units, and a model's length is in metres. Every other length is in the units of the frame it is in: the scene's, or its system's.
  • In the Studio it is the Units section of the Scene tab, and the Studio shows the unit beside each length field (units when the scene names none).
yaml
type: custom:lcards-astrometrics-card
height: 300
# one unit is a km: the Earth below is 6,371 km in radius
units: km
camera:
  views:
    v:
      target: earth
      yaw: 200
      tilt: 15
      distance: 140000
backdrop:
  stars: true
points:
  - id: earth
    xyz: [0, 0, 0]
    label: EARTH
    style: major
objects:
  - type: sphere
    at: earth
    radius: 6371
    image: builtin:earth_blue_marble
  # a geostationary orbit, 42,164 km from Earth's centre
  - type: ring
    radius: 42164
    width: 1
    color: var(--lcars-blue)

  • Each starter is in one set of units:

Every other sceneScene units, built to fit
  • Named units: a part added to a scene (a planet, a belt, a comet, a nebula field, territories, the Badlands, the Nexus, the wormhole) starts at its real size: an Earth-sized planet one au out, the Badlands 10 ly across, the Nexus 1 ly long, the wormhole 40 km long, a comet 0.6 to 35 au from its star (Halley's). None of the three has a size on screen, so 10 ly, 1 ly and 40 km are plausible sizes, not canon.
  • Scene units, with something in the scene: a part is sized to fit: about a quarter of the scene's size (a belt about half, out among the planets). The Nexus added to the Solar system starter is about 80 long, where Neptune's orbit is 160.
  • An empty scene: a part keeps its own sizes.
  • Starbases and asteroids: a starbase and a single asteroid keep their sizes in scene units (a model is drawn larger when it would be too small to see, model_scale). What you type always wins.

One scene in real units ​

Build a scene with the real numbers and the card holds the whole range, from the whole system down to a station in front of Earth. This one opens on Deep Space 9 4 km away, with Earth 42,000 km behind it and the Sun 150 million km further.

  • Depth: the near station, Earth and the Sun draw in the right order. When one depth range cannot hold the view, the card draws it in slices, far to near; a scene built to fit draws in one pass.
  • Distance limits follow the scene: you can zoom in to half its smallest object and out to 20 times its size (a scene built to fit keeps 1 to 100,000). camera.limits.distance sets your own.
  • Fly-to over a long zoom moves evenly in log distance, so each factor of ten takes the same time, and zooming in the camera lines up on its target early instead of sweeping past it close up.
  • What to expect: at true scale, planets are dots from across their system (Earth is 1/23,000 of its orbit), so give them markers and labels. The distance keys still pick what shows when.

More about real units
  • Orbit rings add segments near the camera, so an orbit 150 million km across still passes through its planet seen from 70,000 km (an orbit ring adds them round its body).

Star systems ​

A system object holds points and objects in units of their own, at a point of the scene: the galaxy in light years, Sol in km. One set of units cannot hold a galaxy and a station: at 26,000 light years from the galactic core, numbers in light years are only good to about 30 km.

  • Write each part with the numbers as published: star positions in ly or pc, orbits in AU or km, sizes in km.
  • Ids are card-wide: views, fly-to, rules, templates and paths name a system's points and objects like any others.
  • Distances are in the owner's units: a view's distance is in its target's units (the station view below is 4 km, the galaxy view 140,000 ly), and so are a point's or object's fade, min_distance and max_distance. A view with an [x, y, z] target, and camera.limits, are in scene units.
  • The camera follows: within a few times its size of a system, the camera works in the system's units, so a station a few km across holds steady; further out it works in the scene's. Nothing on screen moves at the change, and a fly-to from the galaxy lands exactly on its target inside.
yaml
type: custom:lcards-astrometrics-card
height: 380
units: ly
camera:
  view: galaxy
  views:
    galaxy:
      target: core
      yaw: 20
      tilt: 35
      distance: 140000
    earth:
      target: earth
      yaw: 225
      tilt: 12
      distance: 70000
    station:
      target: ds9
      yaw: 330
      tilt: 6
      distance: 4
points:
  - id: core
    xyz: [0, 0, 0]
    label: GALACTIC CORE
    style: title
  # tap SOL to dive from the galaxy to Earth
  - id: sol

    marker: dot

More about star systems
  • Galaxy-scale things in the close-ups: a region's walls or a quadrant line thousands of light years long would cross a close-up of a planet. hide_in_systems: true on a point or object hides it while the camera is inside any system (within a few times its size, where it works in the system's units); outside every system it shows as usual. A list of system ids (hide_in_systems: [sol_system]) hides it inside those systems only. The Star Trek galaxy starter sets it on its regions, lines and names.
  • One level: a system cannot hold another system (it reaches from a galaxy down to a station already).
  • Not rotated: a system's axes are the scene's.
  • The disc above: Sol sits 60 ly above the disc, and min_distance: 20 on the grid hides the disc once the camera is inside the system (Distances).

The system object ​

type: system draws nothing itself: it holds points and objects written in its own units, at a point of the scene.

  • units inside a system needs the card's own units, so the card knows how the two relate. For units without a name, give scale instead: how many scene units one unit inside is.
  • A system's objects can be any type but another system.
yaml
type: custom:lcards-astrometrics-card
height: 280
units: au
camera:
  views:
    v:
      target: star
      yaw: 20
      tilt: 35
      distance: 4
points:
  - id: star
    xyz: [0, 0, 0]
    label: STAR
    style: major
objects:
  - type: glow
    at: star
    size: 0.4
    color: var(--lcars-yellow)
  - type: ring
    radius: 1.5
    width: 1
    color: var(--lcars-blue)
  # a planet in km, 1.5 au out
  - type: system
    id: planet_system
    at: [0, 0, 1.5]
    units: km
    points:
      - id: planet
        xyz: [0, 0, 0]
        marker: dot
        label: PLANET
    objects:
      - type: sphere
        at: planet
        radius: 6000
        fill:
          color: var(--lcars-orange)

The real Solar System ​

A point with body: mars sits where Mars really is at the scene date, on its real tilted, elliptical orbit. A sphere with body: mars turns as Mars really does, and a ring with orbit: mars draws the real orbit. All three follow the scene clock.

  • Where: the Sun sits at the origin of the point's frame (the scene, or its system), in that frame's units: set units (km or au; without it au is assumed, with a warning). A moon is placed from the Sun too (its planet plus its own orbit), so it sits by its planet's point. center: jupiter measures from another body instead, for a scene centred on a planet.
  • Axes: y is up toward ecliptic north, and bearing 0 (away from the default camera) is ecliptic longitude 90°, so the planets go round anticlockwise seen from above, as from the north.
  • Spheres and rings: on a sphere, body replaces spin and rotation: its axis is tilted where the real pole points, it turns in the real sidereal day, and its map's longitude 0 is where the prime meridian is. Put the sphere at the body's point (at: mars) with its real radius. A ring with body: saturn lies in Saturn's equator (its rings). An orbit ring for a moon goes at its planet's point (orbit: io, at: jupiter); a planet's needs no at.
  • True sizes: at true scale the planets are dots from across the system, so give their points markers and labels, and fly in to see the bodies.
yaml
type: custom:lcards-astrometrics-card
height: 400
units: au
clock:
  date: now
  rate: 10
camera:
  buttons: true
  view: inner
  views:
    inner:
      target: sun
      yaw: 30
      tilt: 40
      distance: 4.5
points:
  - id: sun
    xyz: [0, 0, 0]
    label: SOL
    style: major
  - id: mercury
    body: mercury
    marker: dot
    label: MERCURY
    style: tiny
  - id: venus
    body: venus
    marker: dot
    label: VENUS
    style: tiny
  - id: earth
    body: earth
    marker: dot
    label: EARTH
  - id: mars
    body: mars
    marker: dot
    label: MARS
objects:
  - type: glow
    at: sun
    size: 0.25
    color: var(--lcars-yellow)
  - type: ring
    orbit: mercury
    width: 1
    opacity: 0.5
  - type: ring
    orbit: venus
    width: 1
    opacity: 0.5
  - type: ring
    orbit: earth
    width: 1
    color: var(--lcars-blue)
  - type: ring
    orbit: mars
    width: 1
    opacity: 0.5
More about the real Solar System
  • Bodies: sun, the planets, pluto and ceres, and the moons moon (Luna), phobos, deimos, io, europa, ganymede, callisto, titan, triton and charon. The Solar system (real positions) starter has them all, with their maps and the asteroid and Kuiper belts.
  • Accuracy: planets from JPL's approximate elements (Standish), within a few hundredths of a degree for the inner planets and a few tenths for the giants, 3000 BC to 3000 AD; outside that they keep going, less exactly. Pluto and Ceres from JPL's small-body elements: a fraction of a degree for Pluto, a degree or two for Ceres outside 2000 to 2050. The Moon from the same series as the sky. The other moons from JPL's mean elements, within a degree from 2000 to 2026 and a few degrees out to 1950 and 2100 (Titan and Phobos up to 15). Rotation from the IAU models. Good for a picture of the sky, not for navigation.

The scene clock ​

clock on the card sets the scene date: where the real bodies are, how they are turned, and the sky dome (sky: sun | moon points and paths) once it is set. It starts at date and runs on at rate.

  • rate is days per second as a number (1 a day a second, 30 a month, 365.25 a year), or with a unit as the time slider shows it: 1 h/s, 30 d/s, 1 y/s, -1 d/s (units s, min, h, d, w, mo, y). live is real time, 0 holds it, and a negative rate runs back.
  • On screen: the time slider gets a date mode on a card with a clock: the date, its rate from a year a second backward to a year a second forward, and NOW. What it sets stays until the card's clock changes.
  • Separate from time_scale: the clock moves the real bodies; time_scale and the speed slider run decorative motion (degree spins, scans, drift, moving dashes). Holding one does not hold the other.
yaml
type: custom:lcards-astrometrics-card
height: 320
units: au
clock:
  # the Apollo 11 landing, an hour a second
  date: "1969-07-20T20:17:00Z"
  rate: 1 h/s
camera:
  buttons: true
  views:
    v:
      target: sun
      yaw: 30
      tilt: 40
      distance: 3.5
points:
  - id: sun
    xyz: [0, 0, 0]
    label: SOL
    style: major
  - id: earth
    body: earth
    marker: dot
    label: EARTH
  - id: venus
    body: venus
    marker: dot
    label: VENUS
    style: tiny
objects:
  - type: glow
    at: sun
    size: 0.25
    color: var(--lcars-yellow)
  - type: ring
    orbit: earth
    width: 1
    color: var(--lcars-blue)
  - type: ring
    orbit: venus
    width: 1
    opacity: 0.5
More about the clock
  • From other cards: tap_action with action: set-clock, card: sol (the astrometrics card's id), date: now and rate: 30 on any LCARdS card; leave out card on the astrometrics card itself.
  • From automations: the lcards.set_clock action, with card_id, date and or rate; date can be a date and time helper's state.
  • From an entity: date and rate can follow an entity (date an input_datetime), or a template (Settings from entities).
  • Spins follow the clock exactly, however fast it runs: at a year a second a planet turns hundreds of times a second and looks still or slow (the wagon-wheel effect). Slow the date down to watch it turn.

clock.datestringnowThe date the scene starts at: now, or an ISO date or date and time (2026-10-30, 1969-07-20T20:17:00Z; a date alone is midnight UTC)
clock.ratenumber / stringliveHow fast the date runs: live, days per second, or with a unit (1 h/s)

Distances ​

Every distance is in the units of what it belongs to. An object's distance is to its nearest edge.

  • A flat grid (shape: plane or disc, not deformed) is measured to its surface, so a galactic disc can hide once you dive into a star system above it: Sol sits 60 ly above the disc in the star systems example, and min_distance: 20 on the grid hides it there.
DistanceUnits
A view's distanceIts target point's: the station view above is 4 km, the galaxy view 140,000 ly
A view with an [x, y, z] targetThe scene's
camera.limitsThe scene's
A point's or object's fade, min_distance, max_distanceIts own system's (the scene's outside one)

In the Studio ​

The Studio edits a system's points and objects in the same Points and Objects tabs: select Open on the system's row. The tabs then show that system's lists, in its units, with a bar naming it and Back to the scene. Tapping a point or object on the preview opens the list it is in.

  • Place and Pick on preview put points on the system's own ground plane, in its units, and drag moves a point on its system's plane.
  • Capture a view while the camera looks at a point (after a fly-to it does): the view names the point, with the distance in its units. New ids are checked against the whole card, since ids are card-wide.
  • Starters adds parts into the system. A part with sizes starts at real sizes in the system's units (an Earth-sized planet one au out in a km system), or sized to fit the system when it has no named units (Units); a part in other units than the system's goes to the scene instead.
