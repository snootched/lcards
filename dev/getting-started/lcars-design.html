Designing LCARS Screens ​

What makes a screen read as LCARS, written down as guidelines you can pick from. They come from an automated analysis of hundreds of recognizable LCARS screens: the Star Trek: Picard season 3 consoles (as rebuilt by meWho's Titan.DS and LCARSlad London) and production panels from The Next Generation, Deep Space Nine and Voyager.

  • A dashboard that follows just a few of these guidelines already looks more like LCARS than one that follows none: pick the ones that suit your screen and taste.
  • Building a Dashboard covers the mechanics (which card for which job, the layout view); this guide is about the look.
  • The Dashboard Gallery has complete screens built from these guidelines.

Two looks ​

LCARS comes in two broad looks: the thin, dark Picard screens and the thick, colourful classic ones. Most of the guidelines below apply to both.

  • The look mostly comes from the theme: the same layout reads as Picard in a Picard theme and as classic in a classic one.
Picard (Picard S3, Titan)Classic (TNG, DS9, Voyager)
StructureThin frames in a few dark, muted tonesThick blocks of colour
ColourMostly black and structure tones; a little accentSeveral pastel colours at once
ButtonsSmall square blocks with code numbers, pills at the edgesGrids of rounded pills, led by big numbers
Main displayLine-drawn schematic inside an open sweepContent inside a bracket or sweep frame
Themes that suit itLCARS Picard [LCARdS Red/Blue Accent], LCARS Picard I/II, LCARS 25CLCARS Default, LCARS TNG, LCARS Classic, LCARS Nemesis Blue

Colour comes from your theme ​

Don't pick colours for an LCARS screen; pick roles, and let the theme supply the colour. Then the screen follows when you switch themes, into light or dark mode, and into alert mode.

  • Prefer the theme: tokens. They resolve to your HA-LCARS theme's variable when it's installed and fall back to the LCARdS palette when it isn't, so the card always has a colour. A bare var(--lcars-...) is empty without HA-LCARS: give it a fallback, as in the table.
  • Keep accent colour sparse. In the Picard references only about a tenth of the screen is accent colour; the rest is black and structure tones. A block stays a structure colour unless it means something.
  • Classic screens are more colourful: several roles side by side, mixed freely. The classic themes already set their roles to the pastel set.
  • HA-LCARS themes have one accent role, so the data colour is a deliberate pick from the palette: check it reads on your theme's structure colours.
yaml
type: custom:lcards-layout-card
layout:
  height: 64px
  grid-gap: 6px
  grid-template-columns: repeat(4, minmax(0, 1fr))
  grid-template-rows: minmax(0, 1fr)
cards:
  # main structure
  - type: custom:lcards-button
    preset: panel-light
    min_height: 0
    text:
      label:
        content: PRIMARY
    style:
      card:
        color:
          background:
            default: theme:colors.ui.primary
  # secondary structure
  - type: custom:lcards-button
    preset: panel-light
    min_height: 0
    text:
      label:
        content: SECONDARY
    style:
      card:
        color:
          background:
            default: theme:colors.ui.secondary
  # dark structure, with its text partner

More about colour
  • Light and dark mode belong to the theme. If your theme has both, a screen built from roles works in both.
  • A view can choose its own theme: theme: LCARS Default on a view shows that view in that theme, whatever the user's profile theme is. Handy for a classic screen on an otherwise Picard dashboard.
  • Every colour format: Colours lists what a colour value can be and how theme tokens resolve.

Colours by state or by hand ​

Decide before you build whether a control's colour follows its entity's state or is picked by hand, because changing your mind later means editing every card. The gallery and the patterns pick by hand; a working dashboard usually shouldn't.

  • By state (the usual choice for your own dashboard): leave the colour unset on a button bound to an entity, and its preset takes the theme's colours for on, off and unavailable. Every switch reads the same way, your theme and alert mode recolour it, and there is nothing to keep in step. Add a state map only where one entity needs colours of its own.
  • By hand: each control gets its own role for on and off (active and inactive), which gives the many-coloured canon look. Every bound card then carries its own colours, so moving to state colours later means cleaning up each one.
  • Mixed: the decorative parts (sweeps, bars, code blocks, cascades) take roles picked by hand, which is where most of the look comes from, and the controls keep the theme's state colours.
  • Why the samples pick by hand: their entities are test helpers, so hand-picked roles are the only way to show the look. Treat their colours as a picture of the style, not as how your switches should be coloured.
yaml
type: custom:lcards-layout-card
layout:
  height: 48px
  grid-gap: 6px
  grid-template-columns: repeat(3, minmax(0, 1fr))
  grid-template-rows: minmax(0, 1fr)
cards:
  # decorative block: a role picked by hand
  - type: custom:lcards-button
    preset: panel-light
    text:
      label:
        content: 03-118
    style:
      card:
        color:
          background: theme:colors.ui.secondary
  # bound control, by state: no colour set, the theme's on and off colours
  - type: custom:lcards-button
    preset: panel-light
    entity: light.living_room
    text:
      label:
        content: LIGHTS
    tap_action:
      action: toggle
  # bound control, by hand: its own role for on and off
  - type: custom:lcards-button
    preset: panel-light
    entity: light.living_room
    text:
      label:
        content: LIGHTS
    style:
      card:
        color:
          background:
            active: theme:colors.ui.primary
            inactive: theme:colors.card.bottomColor
    tap_action:
      action: toggle

Structure ​

An LCARS screen is black, framed by sweeps, and filled with structure rather than empty space. These hold for both looks.

  • Black canvas. Content sits on black inside frames, not on coloured blocks.
  • Sweeps make the frames. A frame is a bar that turns 90° into another bar (an elbow). Two sweeps facing each other (a header pair, or a left and a right sweep) is the signature LCARS shape; a lone elbow in a corner is rarer.
  • Frame the display with an open sweep. On Picard screens the main display sits in one thin sweep that runs along the top, down the left and back along the bottom, open on the right (display sweep); classic ones put a thin sweep inside a thick one. A fully closed or double frame is rare: use it only when asked.
  • Break long bars into segments of different lengths, separated by small black gaps. A segment can carry a small label or number.
  • One gap size everywhere. The same gap between blocks, segments and frames on a screen (grid-gap on the layout view and layout cards). Blocks never touch.
  • Labels sit right-aligned, usually at the bottom right of their block, in condensed capitals (the theme font). Numbers right-aligned in columns.
  • One title, top right, large, in the accent colour or white; a dim subtitle at the bottom right if you need one. Classic titles often read NAME • NUMBER.
  • Numbers as texture. Code-like numbers (13-650, 0099•06) fill blocks and columns, and dense number cascades fill spare areas. Real readings and decorative numbers look alike; that's part of the look.
  • A sweep is at least as wide as what it frames. If a sweep's vertical bar is narrower than the column above it, it looks detached; close a narrow column with a segmented bar instead.
  • Fill space with structure, not black. If an area is empty, put blocks, a number cascade or a segmented bar in it.

Scale and density ​

An LCARS screen is many small parts of fixed size around one large part that takes the rest. Measured on the reference screens, this matters more than colour. Generated screens most often look fan-made because their parts are too big and too few.

  • Small parts keep a fixed size. Give their grid tracks a size in px (64px, 24px) and leave minmax(0, 1fr) for the main display. With every track at 1fr, blocks and pills grow with the screen and turn into large slabs. In a column or bar that must reach the screen edge, let one part (the last sidebar block, one long bar segment) take what is left.
  • Many parts. A full screen carries about 50 to 90 small blocks, pills and bars, plus thin lines, ticks and number rows. A screen with fewer than about 30 reads as a web dashboard painted in LCARS colours.
  • No large empty patch. About a sixth of the screen is plain black, spread out as gaps; no single empty region is larger than about a tenth of the screen. A chart or a short list alone in a big frame breaks this. Put readouts, a scale, or a column of blocks beside it.
  • Three or four size tiers, no medium-large. One large display, one large title, small blocks and pills, and tiny labels, ticks and codes.

type: custom:lcards-layout-card

More about scale and density
  • Measured sizes. Measured at 1600 px wide on Picard and classic references, the typical block is about 2.5% of the screen height and 4% of the width (about 64 x 24 px). Letters in a block are about half the block's height: font_size 13 to 14 in a 24 px block. The title's letters are 3 to 4% of the screen height (Picard) or 6 to 7% (classic). Take these as targets to aim near, not limits.
  • A starting grid. For the display with side columns at 1600 px wide: grid-template-columns: 116px 134px minmax(0, 1fr) 196px (pills, a two-wide block column, the display, a readings column) and grid-template-rows: 84px minmax(0, 1fr) 132px (header, display, strip). For the classic data screen: grid-template-columns: 168px 200px minmax(0, 1fr) 156px 156px and grid-template-rows: 84px 142px 18px 18px 90px minmax(0, 1fr), the first row sized for the title (about 1.3 times its font_size) and the two 18 px rows holding the upper and lower sweeps' bars. Classic pills are about 30 px tall at this size. Other archetypes keep the same idea: fixed px for every area but the display.
  • Overlap by lines, not areas. Each named area must be one rectangle, or the browser drops the whole template (the config checker reports it). Where a part sits inside a sweep's open corner (the classic number rows, a display tucked under a curve), place it with view_layout grid-row and grid-column instead of an area, with a left margin that clears the sweep's inner curve. Columns that must line up (a ruler of blocks beside its numbers) need the same row sizes.
  • Edge columns are narrow. Picard block columns, gauge pairs and pill columns are each about 4 to 9% of the width and sit tight against the frame. A classic sidebar is thicker, about 8 to 12%.
  • Thin structure fills black without weight. 1 to 3 px rules, leader lines from a block to what it labels, tick scales and dot rows are everywhere on the references.
  • Frames interlock. A header bar runs into a sweep that becomes a column, and the column ends in another bar. Regions are divided by thin frames and small gaps, not by one large gap between equal boxes.
  • The main display is busy. A schematic with labels and leader lines, or a chart with a scale and readouts beside it; not a chart alone.
  • Phones and portrait panels hold fewer parts (about half), at the same small sizes.

Picard ​

Picard screens are thin frames in dark tones, with columns of small blocks and a schematic in the middle.

  • Columns of small number blocks down one or both sides: mostly structure tones, a few in the accent, some dark (inactive). See code-block column.
  • A utility column at the far edge: a bracket frame open on the outer side, square-ended there, holding three to five small dark pills (zoom, settings, dimmer) with a red alert pill at the bottom. Every other button is a rectangular block. See utility pills.
  • Concentric sweeps: a thick sweep with a thin line running parallel inside it, for headers and corners. See concentric sweep.
  • Controls inside a wide arm: a thick frame whose black inset holds a group of blocks. See controls inside a wide arm.
  • Vertical gauges with tick scales beside the number columns (Picard and gauge sliders). See gauge bank.
  • The main display is a schematic: a line drawing with leader lines to labels, small accent highlights for faults (MSD). A leader leaves its label from the side, runs out and turns once into the part with a tight curve, never a square corner. A screen keeps one style for every label: that one, a thin bar beside each label whose line leaves the bar's corner and runs straight (level with its part, or one sharp turn into a diagonal), or a short stub and an angled run. See labelled schematic.
  • A strip of tabs and sliders along the bottom. Tabs show their state as a line along the top (toggle tab strip).
  • Fixed chrome, changing centre. Titan.DS keeps the same frame, columns and strips on every screen and swaps only the central display. In LCARdS: a layout view whose centre area holds several cards with visibility conditions, one shown at a time.

Classic (TNG, DS9, Voyager) ​

Classic screens build their structure from thick blocks of colour, with grids of pills led by big numbers.

  • The data screen is the classic shape. An upper band about a quarter of the height carries a sweep, a readings table of tiny labelled values (one or two colours a row), a few pills and the big title, which keeps clear of the readings. A thin band of two to four segments per bar divides it from the lower part. The lower part is a thick sidebar of stacked blocks beside one large line-art display (a grid, scan or schematic). See the data screen archetype.
  • Colour blocks are the structure. Thick header blocks, often in several roles side by side.
  • Grids of rounded pills, each with a small code label, in column groups that alternate two or three roles. See pill grid.
  • A column of big numbers (20, 008, 358) leads each group of pills.
  • Bracket frames: a top and a bottom bar with rounded ends, and a sidebar of stacked blocks with code numbers. See bracket frame.
  • A split header: two sweeps meeting across the top, with a thin segmented band in the gap between them. See split header.
  • Schematics list their labels in columns beside the drawing, and each leader sweeps round into its part with a broad curve, like a small sweep. See labelled schematic.
  • Segment the long arms. A classic bar seldom runs unbroken across the screen: the sweep turns into a short bar, then segments of different lengths and roles carry labels. See segmented bar.

Motion and alerts ​

Keep an LCARS screen mostly still: small things move, and an alert changes everything at once.

  • At rest, move small things only: ticking numbers, a dot row, a scanning line. On the reference screens about one twentieth of the screen moves at a time. See Animations and data grid cascades.
  • Animate entrances once (on_show with draw, cascade or text-reveal), not continuously.
  • Alerts change the whole screen at once: alert mode recolours everything in place, and an alert overlay adds the alert symbol. Avoid making every card flash.
  • Show state by colour in place, not by moving things: a block turns to the accent, a tab's line lights up.

Screen archetypes ​

Start a screen by picking its overall shape, then fill the areas with patterns. These are the layouts that recur across the reference screens, and each suits a different job.

  • An archetype isn't tied to a look: any of them works as a Picard or a classic screen, whatever the table's examples name. The gallery's environmental screen is a data screen in the Picard look.
  • Take groupings and sections from the gallery and the references freely, but compose the screen for its own job rather than reproducing one whole.
  • Two things vary a screen more than anything else: which archetype you start from, and which patterns you put in its areas. A screen that reuses one gallery screen's grid with new labels is the same screen; one that combines a different archetype with two or three patterns is a new one.

Putting it together ​

A screen is a layout view shaped by one of the archetypes, with patterns in its areas. For example: a facing-sweeps header across the top, a code-block column and utility pills down the left, a display sweep round an MSD or chart in the centre, and a toggle tab strip along the bottom. Or a portrait panel: a header bar with a title, a gauge bank, a selector strip.

The Dashboard Gallery has seven complete screens built this way, four in the Picard look and three classic, each laid out differently.
